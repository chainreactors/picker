The worst of the patchpocalypse may be behind us, according to Linus Torvalds.

Writing in his [weekly kernel update post](https://lkml.iu.edu/2611.1/01530.html), the Linux kernel dev leader delivered Linux 7.4 rc2 – the second rough cut of the next kernel – which he rated as a big release for two reasons.

One is that he left some code out of rc1, because he was busy and because it was already the largest such release in the history of the kernel.

REG AD

The other is what Torvalds described as “various AI fixes.”

REG AD

Such fixes are necessary because ever since Anthropic launched its Mythos bug-finding model in [April](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/04/08/anthropic-mythos-model-can-find-and-exploit-0-days/5224393), developers have been able to find more software flaws than was previously possible.

Microsoft, for example, [addressed a record 974 flaws](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/09/09/microsoft-breaks-patch-tuesday-record-with-974-cve-deluge/5295160) in September – its third record fixing spree this year after it [whacked 622 flaws in July](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/07/14/patchpocalypse-now-microsoft-tops-last-months-record-with-622-patch-tuesday-cves/5271434) and 206 in June. Oracle [addressed 1,449 flaws](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/07/23/oracle-drops-1449-security-patches-like-its-the-new-normal/5277114) in a single month.

That’s many more patches than IT teams are set up to handle, yet they must find ways to deal with bugs more quickly because attackers are increasingly [targeting flaws found by AI](https://www.theregister.com/patches/2026/10/19/security-patches-need-patches-now-that-attackers-have-ai/5303927).

Torvalds’ post suggests that, perhaps, the worst of this is behind us.

“This time around Alexei claims that on the bpf side at least the fixes are now coming in faster than the new reports, and that sounds lovely if true,” he wrote.

That’s a reference to Alexei Starovoitov, co-creator and lead dev on the Extended Berkeley Packet Filter (eBPF), the [project](https://www.theregister.com/software/2025/03/09/ebpf-it-doesnt-stand-for-anything-but-it-might-mean-bank/1495487) that makes it possible to run sandboxed programs within the Linux kernel.
