The [Investigatory Powers Tribunal](https://investigatorypowerstribunal.org.uk/judgement/1-mr-vincent-kearney-2-british-broadcasting-corporation-v-1-chief-constable-of-the-police-service-of-northern-ireland-2-chief-constable-of-durham-constabulary-3-security-service-4-governme/) (IPT) has confirmed that the UK government served Apple with a secret Technical Capability Notice (TCN), rejecting the Home Office's argument that acknowledging its existence would damage national security.

The order has been widely reported as demanding access to end-to-end encrypted iCloud data.

The three judges pointed to extensive public reporting, statements by US officials, and media briefings they concluded had been authorized by the UK government.

REG AD

The Home Office had previously adopted its "neither confirm nor deny" (NCND) policy, as is standard when discussing national security matters.

REG AD

The story broke in February 2025, when The Washington Post reported that [the Home Office had demanded an iCloud backdoor](https://www.theregister.com/security/2025/02/07/uk-home-office-stays-shtum-on-alleged-apple-backdoor-order/1394179) the previous month.

A TCN is a notice issued under the Investigatory Powers Act 2016, which compels recipients, such as tech companies, to implement technical changes that facilitate the UK government's legal surveillance powers.

For Apple, a company that has long made privacy central to its marketing, and famously [rejected an FBI order to break open an iPhone](https://www.theregister.com/security/2020/01/14/apple-calls-bs-on-fbi-ag-were-totally-not-dragging-our-feet-in-murder-probe-iphone-decryption-ps-no-backdoors/271787) to assist a terror probe, being served the TCN meant it either had to somehow defy a legal order issued by the UK, or undermine the encryption technology that customers expected.
