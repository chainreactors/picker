@ ALL,

We should first ask an ages old question about tools,

“How to make a tool of use, yet safe enough to use?”

The answer is as far as history goes almost always a compromise with utility tending to lead safety.

With utility all to often expressed as a profit equation, hence we use terms like “Force Multiplier”.

And judge safety not by any real measure, but an argument that all to often “blames the user” not the tool designer or owner.

Thus a counter argument arises that a tool is only to be used by a trained and experienced operator. With the issue of how a person goes from naive to experienced not covered as it’s a cost an owner is unlikely to want to pay.

Injuries went up and up and it was not until uninvolved others were regularly injured that lawmakers felt they had to step in due to adverse press. The most visible cause being Victorian Boiler Explosions that had a habit of spreading broken and mangled bodies of the otherwise uninvolved across large areas.

Eventually we got health and safety legislation that many still see as an unwarranted invasion on their ability to make unrestricted profit. Thus one response being to move factories into other jurisdictions that had lesser or no Health and Safety legislation[1].

As each new “force multiplier” comes into existence it’s first test is utility, the second how to profit from it. Some long way down the road comes consideration for “harms to society” and if legislation is required.

With AI we are not really into the question of,

“Does it have sufficient utility?”

Let alone the question of,

“Does it provide a return on investment?”

So arguably talking about “safety legislation” is “too soon”, because,

**“We have absolutely no way to know what the real harms of AI are going to be.”**

We can make predictions from history but,

1, Do they apply?
2, What about what we’ve not predicted / given credibility to?
