Tim Beasley, a senior incident response consultant at Semperis, was investigating a cyberattack involving Russia’s Midnight Blizzard when a package containing nesting dolls and other regional gifts showed up on his doorstep.

At the time, Beasley served as the lead investigator for Microsoft’s Detection and Response Team (DART). He assumed it was from the group, which has been linked to the Kremlin’s Foreign Intelligence Service (SVR), although the sender didn’t leave a note.

“Just little souvenirs and gift basket stuff from that area of the world,” he told The Register. “We-know-where-you-are kind of stuff. It’s a real eye-opener when you get that type of thing.”

REG AD

While digital intrusions having physical consequences is nothing new, cybercriminals in recent years have turned to [real-life harm](https://www.theregister.com/security/2025/12/28/how-the-human-harms-of-cybercrime-shook-the-world-in-2025/2700062) and [mental and physical trauma](https://www.theregister.com/security/2024/01/18/ransomware-causes-mental-physical-trauma-to-security-pros/1227596) to force victims into paying extortion demands. Some groups even use cybercrime as a gateway drug to real-life violence.

REG AD

“We’ve seen violence as a service get used,” Beasley said.

He’s also investigated ransomware attacks where C-suite executives receive pictures of their kids’ schools directly from the cybercriminals in an attempt to apply psychological pressure to pay the extortion fee.
