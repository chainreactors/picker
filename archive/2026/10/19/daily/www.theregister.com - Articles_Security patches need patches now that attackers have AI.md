Updating your software only gets you so far. A scary scenario - security fixes themselves being bypassed - is becoming more common, according to AI penetration-testing startup Terra Security, which found a sharp rise in reported patch failures in 2026 after years of relatively low numbers.

And no, it’s not a coincidence that [AI vulnerability discovery](https://www.theregister.com/patches/2026/05/14/welcome-to-the-vulnpocalypse-as-vendors-use-ai-to-find-bugs-and-patches-multiply-like-rabbits/5240027) and [patching](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/09/16/mythos-has-made-2026-patching-hell-it-might-make-2027-a-breeze/5296747) also exploded this year.

For example, Russian government cyberspies [exploited a zero-day](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/02/10/microsofts-valentines-gift-to-admins-6-zero-day-fixes/4979681) Windows Shell security bypass bug, CVE-2026-21510, at least a month before Microsoft patched the flaw in February. Redmond’s patch, however, was incomplete, and left a second flaw - a zero-click authentication coercion vulnerability (CVE-2026-32202) - that was [also found and abused](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/04/29/microsoft-patch-fell-short-new-windows-flaw-exploited/5227153) before Microsoft fixed it in April.

REG AD

Terra researchers reviewed every published CVE record and GitHub-reviewed advisory looking for broken fixes, and [found a distinct pattern](https://www.terra.security/blog/how-security-patches-became-maps-for-attackers). After six years of relatively few patch bypasses, this changed in 2026: “A fix would ship, a bypass would be found, and another fix would go out.”

REG AD

The share of GitHub security fixes that required a follow-up advisory reporting a failed patch within 90 days remained at about 0.2 percent between 2019 and 2025. This year, this figure had jumped to 2.3 percent, according to the researchers.

Meanwhile, vulnerabilities whose fixes were later bypassed were about four times as likely to appear on the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency's (CISA's) Known Exploited Vulnerabilities list as other bugs affecting the same vendors, according to Terra.

Of these, server-side request forgery fixes and prototype-pollution patches break the most. Compared with their share of all CVEs, prototype-pollution vulnerabilities were about 11 times as common among broken-fix CVEs, while server-side request forgery vulnerabilities were 4.6 times as common, according to Terra.

This has to do with the way these types of bug classes are fixed: using a reject-list of inputs such as hostnames, paths, and object keys. There’s a “simple” reason for this, according to Terra.
