Your MSSP Added 10 New Clients. What Breaks First?

Adding 10 new clients sounds like exactly the kind of problem an [MSSP](https://any.run/mssp/?utm_source=anyrunblog&utm_medium=article&utm_campaign=mssp-growth&utm_term=151026&utm_content=linktomssplanding) wants to have. More revenue, more accounts, more room to grow.

But growth also brings more alerts, suspicious files and URLs, investigations, escalations, reports, and SLA commitments. And if too much of that work still depends on manual analyst effort, those 10 new clients can put pressure on the SOC faster than expected.

That is usually where the cracks start to show: analysts spend more time on each case, escalations pile up, and keeping the same level of service gets harder.

## 1. Alert Volume Grows Faster Than Analyst Capacity

The first pressure usually shows up in the investigation queue.

Every new client adds another stream of alerts to the SOC. Some are easy to dismiss, while others send analysts into a longer process: opening files, checking URLs, following redirects, collecting IOCs, and figuring out whether the activity is actually malicious.

The workload grows especially fast when analysts have to keep stepping in just to move an investigation forward. A phishing page may hide the next stage behind a CAPTCHA, a QR code, or another interaction. If the analysis stops there, someone has to take over manually before the team can see what comes next.

### Let Automation Handle the Routine Steps, Then Bring Analysts in When Needed

A lot of the time spent on phishing and malware analysis comes from small steps that keep interrupting the investigation.

The investigation keeps moving instead of stopping every time the attack requires another click or action.

## 2. Investigation Context Becomes the Hidden Time Sink

As the client base grows, analysts spend more time not only investigating threats but also figuring out what the indicators around them actually mean.

A suspicious domain, IP, hash, or URL may need to be checked against several sources before the analyst can tell whether it is part of a known campaign, tied to malicious infrastructure, or connected to attacks seen elsewhere.

### See the Bigger Picture Around the Indicator

A single IOC rarely tells the whole story. Analysts need to know what sits around it: related domains, IPs, URLs, malware, infrastructure, and other attacks where the same indicators appeared.
