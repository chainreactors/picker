Welcome, once again, to PWNED, where we talk smack about organizations that have smacked themselves down by practicing poor security hygiene. This week's story involves everyone's favorite low-tech password manager: the sticky note.

Our terrifying tale comes courtesy of Christian Espinosa, a certified penetration tester with over 30 years of experience and the CEO of [Blue Goat Cyber](https://bluegoatcyber.com/), a security company. Espinosa shared an experience where critical credentials were right out in the open for any person in the office to see.

During a walkthrough of a client's office, he noticed a password written on a sticky note that was taped to an employee's monitor. While writing down your password and placing it in a physically secure paper notebook might be ok, taping it to the computer is definitely not.

REG AD

"Before I get my fingers on any system, I first walk the physical area that the system is located in — attackers do that when they're looking at a target too," Espinosa explained. “Physical security vulnerabilities are neglected much more often than technical security vulnerabilities and firms invest in firewalls where a sticky note is placed 3 feet from the front door."

REG AD

Espinosa took a photo of the sticky note and immediately tried logging into that employee's account. Success. Then, he tried the very same password on the VPN, which apparently had been maintained by that employee.

Once he connected to the VPN, Espinosa had full access to the company network and all of its shared drives. He opened a shared drive that this employee had access to and there was an HR folder with all kinds of personally identifiable information (PII) about employees, including home addresses, driver's licenses, and scanned W-4 forms. There were even spreadsheets that listed everyone's salaries.

The employee account also had access to a document that contained a list of internal server names and the names of the people in charge of each server (great for phishing). Another document had instructions for how to log into the accounting server specifically.

The accounting server was a particular treasure trove of client information. On that server, Espinosa found a folder with vendor banking information, invoices, and payment records.
