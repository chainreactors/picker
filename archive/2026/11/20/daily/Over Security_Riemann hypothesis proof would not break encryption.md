A proof of the Riemann hypothesis would not break RSA, elliptic-curve cryptography or Bitcoin keys. Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge [said on 20 November 2026](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf) that he and the AI model Claude had found one, and released it as computer-checkable Lean code that other mathematicians are still verifying. Encryption rests on different hard problems, and a proof gives an attacker no shortcut for any of them.

### In brief

* The proof is unverified. Terence Tao describes it as an enormous proof that exists only as Lean code and expects it to take some time to digest.
* RSA depends on large numbers being hard to factor, and elliptic-curve cryptography on a problem called the discrete logarithm. Neither assumes the Riemann hypothesis.
* The software that generates encryption keys uses primality tests whose guarantees need no unproven hypothesis.
* If the hypothesis is true, every algorithm that relies on it already works today. A proof adds certainty for mathematicians and no new capability for attackers.
* The scheduled threat to RSA and elliptic curves is the quantum computer. NIST plans to deprecate both after 2030 and disallow them after 2035.

## What has been claimed about the Riemann hypothesis?

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, says he believes he has proved the Riemann hypothesis and its generalised form while working with the company’s Claude model. The note on his website dates the work from 6 October to 16 November 2026 and credits Claude with much of the technical progress, saying the model “far outpaced me near the finish line”. Instead of a conventional paper, the proof is a Lean archive split into fourteen files, and the note refers to an informal written version of more than 250,000 pages.

The Riemann hypothesis dates from 1859 and concerns the zeros of the zeta function, which control how evenly prime numbers are spread among the integers. It is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, each carrying a $1 million prize from the Clay Mathematics Institute. The generalised version extends the same statement to a wider family of functions.
