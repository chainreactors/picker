UK police forces must do more to prevent the wrongful arrests of people subjected to retrospective facial recognition (RFR) searches, according to a Criminal Justice Inspectorate that holds deep concerns about the way in which the technology is used.

According to a [report](https://hmicfrs.justiceinspectorates.gov.uk/publication-html/how-police-forces-and-national-crime-agency-use-retrospective-facial-recognition/) from His Majesty's Inspectorate of Constabulary and Fire & Rescue Services (HMICFRS), there is a large gap in understanding among police forces and the National Crime Agency about how RFR should be used in responsible policing.

The inspectorate blamed "fragmented and underdeveloped" frameworks for governance and legislation.

REG AD

It pointed to a lack of bespoke legislation to provide a uniform approach to police facial recognition use – and said existing laws have not kept pace with the technology. HMICFRS also said the rules governing each force's use of RFR are mismatched, and called for a statutory code to be included in the forthcoming Police Reform Bill to set nationwide standards.

REG AD

Illustrating the breadth of misunderstanding across forces, the report noted that despite the message being routinely communicated by National Police Chiefs' Council (NPCC) reps, individual investigators "often" thought RFR results constituted evidence rather than intelligence.

In many cases, they are unaware that RFR results require further enquiries before enforcement action can be taken.

"We found that some investigators didn't always carry out enough corroborative enquiries before acting on potential matches," the report stated. "In a small number of cases, officers relied too heavily on RFR results, which led to inappropriate investigative action.

"Although some forces had strengthened their processes after such incidents, practice remained inconsistent."

Later in the report, the inspectorate described the findings as "deeply concerning."
