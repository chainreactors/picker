Tragically simple private keys used by a certification authority based in India have been compromised, meaning the 60-million-plus certs it has issued can’t be trusted and necessitating a massive clean-up job.

The CA of concern in this case is called eMudhra, which [boasts](https://emudhra.com/en/our-history) of having issued over 60 million digital certificates to help usher in a world of digital trust.

The company has instead panicked the world.

REG AD

News of the company’s errors appeared on Tuesday in a post by Dennis Jackson, a member of Mozilla’s cryptography engineering team.

REG AD

“On Saturday, October 17, at 20:06 UTC, Mozilla received a report from a community member that private keys associated with eight eMudhra intermediate certificates had been compromised,” he [wrote](https://groups.google.com/a/mozilla.org/g/dev-security-policy/c/0R3XLJL6FuE/m/Kp1v6CPuAAAJ). “These keys could allow attackers to issue fraudulent HTTPS certificates for websites they do not control.”

Or as Cloudflare [put it](https://blog.cloudflare.com/emudhra-certificate-compromise/), “an attacker can create a forged certificate for any domain and use it to impersonate servers to intercept or modify traffic.”

Jackson has since [elaborated on](https://emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/) the incident and the response, which has thankfully seen Mozilla, Chrome, and Apple remediate the issue so their browsers won’t rely on the certificates – at least after users install an update. He also reports that eMudhra has revoked the certs, and lists many FOSS projects that have issued updates that mean they won’t recognize certs from eMudhra.

That’s welcome, but sadly certs signed with these private keys are still out there and many services rely on them. Browser makers are set up to detect and reject bad certs. Others who relied on them may not be, and will likely need to dig into their environments to see if they rely on certs signed by eMudhra. Developer Geoffrey Huntley has [offered some advice](https://ghuntley.com/patch/) on automating that process.

At the time of writing, eMudhra has not responded to *The Register*’s inquiries about the incident and its security practices.

The latter question is of great interest because security researcher Scott Helme[analyzed](https://scotthelme.co.uk/eight-bits-of-trust-emudhras-ca-keys-cracked-in-milliseconds/) eMudhra’s certs and found one the private keys it used “is the number 4.”
