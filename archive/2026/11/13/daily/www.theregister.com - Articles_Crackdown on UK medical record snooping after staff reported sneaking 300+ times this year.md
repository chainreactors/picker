Britain's privacy watchdog has put healthcare bosses on notice after receiving more than 300 reports of staff snooping through medical records this year, demanding that executives personally confirm they're doing enough to keep prying eyes out of patient files.

The Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) said on Thursday that it had [written to more than 200 senior health leaders](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2026/11/uk-health-sector-warned-to-tackle-medical-record-snooping) across the UK, warning that employees accessing records without good reason put patient privacy and public trust at risk.

The ICO received and assessed more than 300 reports of medical record snooping between January and mid-October 2026. Staff were caught looking up people they knew, poking around patient files out of sheer curiosity, and sharing private information with people who had no business seeing it.

The figure covers reports submitted by healthcare organizations rather than confirmed breaches, and excludes cases still awaiting assessment.

REG AD

ICO chief executive Paul Arnold wants health bosses to personally assure him that their organizations have safeguards in place to stop staff having a nose through records they aren't supposed to see.

"Accessing someone's health records without a legitimate reason isn't harmless curiosity. It can cause enormous distress to patients and jeopardize the vital trust people place in our health service," Arnold said.

In a[three-page letter](https://ico.org.uk/media2/3fbfhwa5/protecting-patients-from-unauthorised-access-letter-20261112.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com) to chief executives, Arnold makes clear that simply trusting staff to behave themselves won't cut it. He wants to know who can access patient records, whether anyone is checking what they're looking at, and what happens when somebody gets caught snooping.

Health bosses are also expected to investigate suspected breaches without dragging their feet, work out whether patients need to be told, and take action against staff caught accessing records they shouldn't.

Executives have until November 27 to respond. Those who haven't reviewed their safeguards in the past six months, or aren't happy with what they find, have been given another three months to put their house in order.
