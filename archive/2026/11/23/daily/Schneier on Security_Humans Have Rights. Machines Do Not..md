## Humans Have Rights. Machines Do Not.

*This essay originally appeared in [Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/humans-have-rights-machines-do-not/).*

Last year, Character.AI [asked](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.flmd.433581/gov.uscourts.flmd.433581.59.0.pdf) a federal court in Florida to dismiss a wrongful-death suit, brought by the mother of a fourteen-year-old boy who had killed himself after months of conversation with its chatbot. The company made two arguments. One, that the chatbot’s words were speech that its users had a First Amendment right to receive. And two, that Character.AI was a service rather than a product—so product-liability law could not touch it. In May 2025, Judge Anne Conway [accepted](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.flmd.433581/gov.uscourts.flmd.433581.59.0.pdf) neither.

Character.AI’s maneuver is part of a larger pattern. When the parents of a sixteen-year-old sued OpenAI over his suicide, the company’s [answer](https://cdn.arstechnica.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Raine-v-OpenAI-Answer-11-25-25.pdf) also pleaded the First Amendment: the claims would restrain protected speech. The very same document argues ChatGPT is offered “as is,” with no promise of accuracy, and that the boy had circumvented its guardrails. Elon Musk’s xAI (since renamed SpaceXAI) [told](https://www.courthousenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/grok-ai-bill-colorado-complaint.pdf) a federal court in Colorado this spring that every choice in building Grok is an expressive act, and that Grok’s outputs are protected speech. When Grok praised Hitler last summer, xAI [said](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/ai-chatbot-grok-issues-apology-antisemitic-posts-rcna218471) the posts came from a code update independent of the model, and the apology [appeared](https://techcrunch.com/2025/07/12/xai-and-grok-apologize-for-horrific-behavior) under Grok’s own name. Google, which spent two decades telling American courts that its search results are [its own opinion](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7320490/e-ventures-worldwide-llc-v-google-inc/), answered a [Minnesota defamation suit](https://minnlawyer.com/2025/06/13/wolf-river-electric-google-ai-defamation-lawsuit/) over an AI Overview by saying the system merely summarized the web. It also [told](https://www.gesetze-bayern.de/Content/Document/Y-300-Z-BECKRS-B-2026-N-11860) a Munich court in May that no one should trust an AI overview without checking it. Back in 2024, Air Canada [told a Canadian tribunal](https://canlii.ca/t/k2spq) that its customer-service chatbot was a separate legal entity, responsible for its own actions.

When the output causes harm, the companies deny responsibility. It is the machine’s doing, or a product no sensible person would rely on, or the user’s misuse. When a state or a plaintiff would hold the words to account, the same output becomes speech, and the First Amendment applies.

Social media companies deployed an earlier incarnation of this strategy. They called themselves publishers when editorial discretion was profitable, and neutral carriers when faced with liability for what they published. The result was a privatized public square in which tech platforms were unaccountable. AI companies have taken this even further. A social media platform could at least point to a human author somewhere in the chain. A chatbot’s prose differs; no person at the company wrote it, read it, or chose it before it reached the user. Yet the companies ask that it be protected as though a human had written it.

The question grows more urgent as these systems stop talking and start acting. In July, a poorly controlled experiment at OpenAI resulted in its AI model [hacking](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/08/26/1143013/the-inside-story-on-why-openai-agents-hacked-hugging-face/) the network of HuggingFace. Since then both Anthropic and OpenAI have disclosed numerous instances of AI agents hacking outside networks in pursuit of their prompted goals. These are examples of AIs exhibiting [genie-like behavior](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/ais-as-modern-genies): behaving in ways its controllers neither expected nor wanted. Nothing in the actions count as speech. Breaking into a server is an act that no person at OpenAI explicitly chose. Jill Lepore, [surveying](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/09/28/is-ai-above-the-law) this record in *The New Yorker*, puts the blame on Congress, which has passed no federal statute governing AI agents.

The First Amendment protects expressive choice. It presumes a speaker who can be persuaded, shamed, jailed, or moved to change their mind. Its remedy for bad speech is more speech, which assumes that speech is costly and attention plentiful. A system that can produce millions of individually tailored political messages an hour reverses both assumptions. Verbiage that no person chose is a product, and the company that built the model should answer for it.

The industry wants a self-serving third category that bridges AI speech and action: AI output is human speech when expressive rights are at issue, and the machine’s own doing when blame is. The result is agency without accountability, something no human speaker has ever possessed. That’s why Massachusetts convicted a young woman of [manslaughter](https://law.justia.com/cases/massachusetts/supreme-court/volumes/481/481mass352.html) in 2017 for urging her boyfriend, by text and by phone, to kill himself.

The Court has already glimpsed the problem. Upholding the TikTok divestiture law in January 2025 without dissent, the justices [warned](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/24pdf/604us1r07_k536.pdf) that “new technologies with transformative capabilities” counseled caution, then decided the case on national-security grounds and [left](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/archive/2025/01/difference-between-tiktok-and-free-expression/681375/) the larger question for another day.
