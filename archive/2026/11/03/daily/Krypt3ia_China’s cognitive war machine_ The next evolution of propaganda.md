## China’s cognitive war machine: The next evolution of propaganda

The Chinese have been working on cognitive warfare for years. This is not particularly surprising. The People’s Liberation Army (PLA) has long understood that manipulating what people believe can be just as useful as destroying military equipment. Psychological warfare, propaganda, and disinformation have been around forever. What has changed is the technology available to conduct these operations and the possibility of automating much of the process.

Over the last several years, Chinese military researchers have been publishing papers on using artificial intelligence, large language models (LLMs), and autonomous agents to conduct what they call cognitive-domain operations (认知域作战). The objective is to influence how people perceive events, process information, and make decisions. This goes beyond convincing someone that a particular narrative is true. It includes creating doubt, exploiting existing divisions, undermining confidence in institutions, and manipulating the information environment in which people form their opinions.

The interesting part is how the PLA proposes to accomplish this. Rather than relying entirely on people to create propaganda and distribute it through social media, Chinese researchers are examining systems that could collect information about populations, identify psychological characteristics, generate tailored messaging, and monitor how audiences respond. The results would then feed back into the system to refine subsequent activity. Essentially, they are looking at turning influence operations into a continuous process supported by artificial intelligence.

In 2023, researchers from the PLA Air Force Early Warning Academy published a paper titled *An Exploration of the Effectiveness Mechanisms and Key Technologies of Algorithmic Cognitive Warfare* ([《算法认知战的致效机理与关键技术探析》](https://www.ahdjbh.com/dengbaozixun/997.html)). This paper is particularly useful because it lays out how the researchers believe algorithmic cognitive warfare should work. They describe six stages beginning with profiling the target audience, capturing attention, shaping how information is interpreted, encouraging desired reactions, intervening as necessary, and monitoring the results.

There is nothing particularly revolutionary about the underlying psychological concepts. Intelligence services and propaganda organizations have used variations of these techniques for decades. What makes the Chinese research interesting is the proposed use of algorithms and AI to connect these activities and potentially conduct them at a scale that would be difficult to manage manually.

The paper discusses data fusion, behavioral analysis, deepfakes, recommendation algorithms, and generative models as enabling technologies. The goal is to understand an audience well enough to influence what information it encounters and how it responds. The system would not necessarily need to manufacture false information. It could take existing grievances, political disagreements, or legitimate concerns and amplify them in ways that serve the operator’s objectives.

*This is where the more recent research on LLMs and autonomous agents comes into play.*

## The Advent of LLM Cognitive Warfare

In 2024 and 2026, Chinese military and academic researchers published additional work examining how language models could support cognitive operations. A May 2026 publication from the Chinese Institute of Command and Control describes cognitive warfare moving toward a stage involving large-model cognitive agents. These are systems that could maintain artificial identities, remember previous interactions, participate in conversations, and coordinate with other agents.

Consider the difference between that and a traditional bot network. Most bot networks rely on scripts, scheduled posts, and human operators to manage accounts and messaging. They can be effective, but they also produce recognizable patterns. Accounts repeat the same material, post at unusual intervals, and frequently lack the characteristics of genuine human interaction. An LLM-driven agent could potentially behave differently. It could maintain a consistent persona, respond to conversations, remember what someone said previously, and adjust its language to match the audience. Multiple agents could operate together while appearing to represent different people with different interests and opinions. The result could be an influence operation that looks less like a coordinated propaganda campaign and more like an ordinary argument unfolding across social media.

*That is the theory, anyway.*

Another area of Chinese research makes this more interesting. In February 2026, researchers associated with the PLA Academy of Military Science and National Defense University published papers examining the use of LLM-driven agents to simulate human behavior and social networks. These systems attempt to model how individuals interact, how groups form opinions, and how information spreads through larger populations.

The potential military application is obvious. If you can simulate a population, you might be able to test different influence strategies before using them against real people. Researchers could examine how a particular narrative spreads, which groups respond to it, and whether certain messages appear to produce the desired reactions.

Of course, there is a problem with assuming any of this will work as advertised. Artificial agents are not human beings. LLMs can generate convincing conversations without accurately reproducing human psychology. They hallucinate, behave inconsistently, and frequently reflect the assumptions of their developers and training data. The Chinese researchers acknowledge many of these limitations.

## A simulated population agreeing with your propaganda does not mean a real population will follow along.

Still, the research shows where Chinese military thinking is headed. The PLA is not simply interested in generating more propaganda. Its researchers are examining whether artificial intelligence can help model the people they want to influence and predict how those people might respond.

*There is also a commercial side to this.*

Chinese technology companies have been developing platforms for social-media intelligence, public-opinion monitoring, behavioral analysis, synthetic-media generation, and automated information processing. Some of these capabilities overlap considerably with what the military researchers describe. GoLaxy, also known as Zhongke Tianji (中科天玑), is one company worth examining. Its products include systems for collecting and analyzing social-media information and characterizing online audiences. External reporting based on leaked company materials has described capabilities associated with its GoPro platform that could support AI-assisted influence operations.

Another company, Xianjin AI, openly markets a product called *Advanced Cognitive Warfare* (先进认知战). There are other firms working on public-opinion analysis, automated commentary systems, and military AI applications. This does not mean every company selling these technologies is conducting covert influence operations. Public-opinion monitoring has legitimate commercial and government applications. Neither does the existence of a military LLM automatically mean it is being used to manipulate foreign populations.
