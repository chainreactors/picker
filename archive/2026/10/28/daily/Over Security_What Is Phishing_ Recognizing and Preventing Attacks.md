# What Is Phishing? Recognizing and Preventing Attacks

[![](https://blog.sucuri.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/avatar_user_116_1769198545-60x60.png)](https://blog.sucuri.net/author/sucuriblog)

[Sucuri](https://blog.sucuri.net/author/sucuriblog)

* October 27, 2026

![Phishing](https://blog.sucuri.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/BlogPost_FeatureImage_1490x700_Phishing-DHL-820x386.jpg)

Phishing is an attack in which someone impersonates a person or organization you trust, like your host, registrar, bank, or colleague, in order to steal information, redirect a payment, or talk you into an action that helps them. The message is the delivery vehicle, and the goal is always the next action you take.

If you run a website, you’re on both sides of this problem. Your hosting panel, domain registrar, WordPress admin, email, and payment accounts are all valuable targets, and if an attacker gets in through any of them, your site can quietly end up hosting phishing pages aimed at someone else’s customers.

In this guide we’ll walk through how phishing actually works, the forms it takes, the signals that give it away, what it does to a website’s reputation, and what to do in the first hour after you suspect you’ve been caught. If you want the shorter conceptual primer first, start with our phishing explainer and come back here for the site-owner view.

## How does phishing work?

Nearly every phishing attempt follows the same four beats: a message arrives, the message creates a reason to act, you’re asked to do one specific thing, and the consequence lands somewhere you didn’t expect.

Here’s a quick hypothetical: An email appears to come from your hosting provider with the subject ***“Final notice: account suspension in 24 hours.”*** It says an unpaid invoice will take your site offline tomorrow, and it includes a “**Review billing**” button. The button leads to a page that looks exactly like your host’s login screen, on a domain that’s close to the real one but not the real one. You enter your username, password, and the six-digit code from your authenticator app. The page spins, then redirects you to the genuine host’s website, which looks completely normal. Meanwhile, the attacker has used those credentials and that code in real time to open a session of their own.

Notice what the lure was doing. It wasn’t really about billing. It was about compressing your decision time so you’d type a password on a page you hadn’t checked.

To be clear, receiving a phishing message doesn’t mean anything has been compromised, and clicking a link usually doesn’t either. A click can expose your IP address and browser details, and it can land you on a page that tries to exploit your browser or hand you a file, but on its own a visit to a credential-harvesting page doesn’t give anyone access to your accounts. What matters is whether you submitted something, downloaded something, or approved something. Our walkthrough of a real phishing email in [How to Recognize & Avoid Phishing Emails: A Cautionary Tale](https://blog.sucuri.net/2023/07/how-to-recognize-avoid-phishing-emails-a-cautionary-tale.html) shows how convincing the staging can be when you see it step by step.

## What are the common types of phishing?

It helps to separate two things that often get mixed together: how an attacker targets you, and how the message reaches you.

Targeting is the difference between bulk and bespoke. Mass phishing goes out to huge lists and plays the odds with generic branding, generic urgency, and no idea who you are. Spear phishing is written specifically for you. It names your host, your plugin stack, your agency, or the client whose invoice you’re waiting on. Attackers research spear-phishing targets using whatever is public.
