Visa and Mastercard customer info leaked by the data thieves and extortionists who claim to have stolen it from the financial giants doesn’t include card numbers, passwords, or home addresses.

On Thursday, Visa, Mastercard, and JPMorgan Chase confirmed to The Register that they are [investigating third-party breaches](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/11/19/chase-visa-mastercard-confirm-third-party-cyberattacks-that-may-have-exposed-customers-info/5311175) that may involve customer information.

Ransomware and extortion crew n0n claimed to be responsible for all three, and on Thursday published samples of the stolen files – data-theft groups typically leak some of the compromised data ahead of the official extortion deadline in an effort to put more pressure on victim companies to pay the ransom demand.

REG AD

Researchers at SOCRadar, a threat-intelligence firm that has been [tracking n0n](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/10/30/crim-spent-7k-on-ai-services-in-extortion-attacks-then-left-evidence-for-all-to-see/5306754) since it emerged in September, have parsed all 14 files in the n0n’s published Visa and Mastercard archives, and told us that the files don’t contain any financial details belonging to banking customers.

REG AD

“We found no card numbers, expiry dates, CVVs, passwords, dates of birth, or home addresses in any file,” Ensar Seker, SOCRadar chief information security officer, told The Register. “Visa's statement that no card numbers were affected matches our scan.”

Mastercard previously said that stolen data included a “limited amount of email addresses – non-payments information” from a subset of cardholders in a benefits program.

According to SOCRadar’s analysis, the leaked files do contain mobile numbers, Bank Identification Number (BIN) prefixes, and dated airline, hotel, and restaurant bookings. So while the data doesn’t include card or account numbers that crooks could use to drain customers' bank accounts or rack up massive credit card bills, “that detail is what a fraudster needs to pose as a concierge desk or a bank,” in an email or voice-phishing social engineering attack, Seker said.

It’s also worth noting that the published files only represent about 0.4 percent of the data n0n claims to have hoovered up in the breach: about 4.3 GB and 1.9 million marketing events. “So a further release is possible,” according to Seker.
