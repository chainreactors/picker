# DHS Inspector General Finds That TSA is Not Properly Safeguarding Passenger Facial Recognition Data

![The angular outline of three faces as a computer might see them, colored like a rainbow](https://www.eff.org/files/styles/resized_banner/public/banner_library/whyfacebanner.png?itok=zCMIux-n)

*EFF legal intern Gabrielle Ladun was the principal author of this post.*

The Department of Homeland Security Office of Inspector General (DHS OIG) has [published a summary of findings](https://www.oig.dhs.gov/sites/default/files/assets/2026-09/OIG-26-27-Sep26.pdf) from its audit of the Transportation Security Administration’s (TSA) facial recognition technology. While the detailed findings are classified, the summary states that the OIG’s audit uncovered a “significant weakness” in the technology’s ability to protect the privacy of passenger data. This has massive privacy implications for the millions of passengers who travel and don’t opt out of the TSA’s biometric surveillance technology verification process.

TSA uses facial comparison technology for identity verification of airline passengers. A camera takes a photo of passengers to verify that their provided identification matches their live photo. Although TSA states that it is “[committed to protecting traveler privacy](https://www.tsa.gov/news/press/factsheets/facial-comparison-technology),” the audit highlights that this has not been the case.

The OIG found that TSA’s Credential Authentication Technology 2’s (CAT-2) vendor “was able to access and extract sensitive passenger information, including driver’s license and passport images, during system troubleshooting and enhancements, without TSA’s knowledge.”

The OIG further admonished the agency:

> TSA lacked clear policies, did not track when this data was extracted, could not verify that extracted information was properly deleted, and did not notify passengers when their biometric and biographic information may have been stored. As a result, although passenger information was generally protected during routine operations, weaknesses in vendor oversight created a risk that sensitive personal information could be retained or exposed.

We hope the TSA will fix these issues. The OIG summary states:

> We issued one finding and three recommendations to strengthen controls, improve oversight, and ensure transparency and the proper handling of passenger data. TSA concurred with all three recommendations. Two recommendations are open and resolved, and one remains open and unresolved.

### **Why EFF Opposes the Government’s Use of Facial Recognition Technology**

EFF [has a long history](https://www.eff.org/document/ban-government-use-facial-recognition) of opposing governmental use of facial recognition technology.
