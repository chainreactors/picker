# Event Recap: Forensics Europe Expo (FEE) 2026

21st July 2026 by [Forensic Focus](https://www.forensicfocus.com/author/forensicfocus/ "View all posts by Forensic Focus")

![](https://www.forensicfocus.com/stable/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/1000023975-scaled.jpg)

[Forensics Europe Expo](https://www.forensicseuropeexpo.com/?utm_source=partner_forensicfocus) returned to Olympia London on 1 and 2 July 2026 for two busy days of talks, demonstrations and networking, and the digital forensics stream covered plenty of ground. A couple of threads in particular stood out: the ever-expanding list of everyday objects that can quietly hold evidence — video doorbells, smart toys, games consoles — and the growing pressure that AI-generated media places on how far digital evidence can be trusted. Both served as a useful reminder of how quickly the field is shifting — and, judging by the exhibitor floor, of how hard the tools are working to keep pace. With [next year’s event](https://www.forensicseuropeexpo.com/?utm_source=partner_forensicfocus) already confirmed for 22–23 June 2027 at ExCeL London, there will be plenty more to discuss when the sector meets again.

## Meeting the Exhibitors

As in previous years, we were there with a stand of our own — a welcome chance to meet readers and catch up with advertisers in person, and to hand out plenty of merch: baseball caps, pens, bags, laptop stickers and a few other bits. If you picked something up, it was good to see you; if you missed out, do say hello next year!

![](https://www.forensicfocus.com/stable/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/20260702_120052-scaled.jpg)

Beyond our own corner, the hall was worth as much time as the conference programme, with more than 60 exhibitor stands.

It was great to see Leica Geosystems, recent guests on the [Forensic Focus podcast](https://www.forensicfocus.com/podcast/leicas-marcus-rowe-on-investigating-the-worlds-largest-crash-test-plus-what-to-expect-at-fee-2026/), whose forensic digital twin work also featured in the conference programme (more on that below). We also spent time with the team at [Detego](http://www.detegoglobal.com/), and stopped by [MSAB](https://www.msab.com/) — who, as well as delivering one of the standout talks of the digital stream, quietly took the unofficial prize for best stand giveaway: a multifunctional pen doubling as a torch, stylus, screwdriver and spirit level. A fitting piece of kit for a company whose message that day was all about looking beyond the obvious tool for the job.

![](https://www.forensicfocus.com/stable/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/20260702_122250-768x1024.jpg)

![](https://www.forensicfocus.com/stable/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/1000023987-700x1024.png)

Other notable stands included Addressable, where it was a pleasure to talk to Dan and hear about what they do. Their platform, Spectra, tackles a problem familiar to anyone working crypto cases: conventional blockchain analytics can trace how funds move, but not who sits behind a wallet — so Addressable adds an off-chain layer of “device intelligence” that links wallet addresses to the real-world devices behind them, picking up the trail where on-chain tools run cold. [Amped Software](https://ampedsoftware.com/), whose image and video authentication work fed directly into the deepfake session, and IntaForensics, the digital forensics firm behind the doorbell talk, both helped round out a busy and productive floor.

## Everyday Objects as Evidence

The talks were where the show really earned its place in the calendar, and the digital forensics stream in particular threw up some genuinely useful sessions. A clear theme emerged across several of them: the sheer, sprawling range of ordinary consumer devices that now hold evidential value — and how easy they are to overlook.

Carl Osborne and Jake Blythe of IntaForensics set the tone early with “Decoding the Doorbell.” Their argument was a simple one with big implications: video doorbells have quietly assembled themselves into a vast, decentralised surveillance network, one house at a time. That footage regularly ends up in investigations, but it cannot be taken at face value. The pair walked through the hidden data lifecycles behind smart-doorbell footage, and what it actually takes to move from a raw clip pulled off a consumer device to material that will stand up as evidence in court — the interrogation, authentication and understanding that has to happen in between.

The same “look beyond the obvious” spirit ran through Adam Newberry’s talk from the University of Staffordshire, “The Curious Case of the Singing Cactus.” On paper, a novelty toy that repeats what you say back to you sounds like the least serious thing at a forensics show. In practice, Newberry’s dissection of how the device records, stores and retains audio was one of the most quietly thought-provoking sessions of the two days.
