# AI-Assisted Paper on Z-Library Cites a TorrentFreak Article That Doesn’t Exist

today by
[Ernesto Van der Sar](https://torrentfreak.com/author/ernesto/)

[Home](https://torrentfreak.com "Go to TorrentFreak.") > [Opinion Articles](https://torrentfreak.com/category/opinion/ "Go to the Opinion Articles category archives.") >

A fresh academic paper on Z-Library cites a TorrentFreak article that doesn’t exist, and several other sources in the paper are also unfindable. The authors disclose that they used AI tools, which may explain what is going on. The use of AI in an academic paper on shadow libraries is also somewhat ironic, as rightsholders are suing AI companies for using these sites to train AI models.

![robot facepalm](https://torrentfreak.com/images/robot-facepalm-600x413.png)[Z-Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z-Library) is one of the largest pirate ebook libraries on the Internet, offering millions of books and papers for free.

For students and researchers who can’t afford expensive textbooks and journal subscriptions, it can be a useful resource. At the same time, publishers have spent years trying to take it down, with [takedown notices and blocking orders](https://torrentfreak.com/publishers-ramp-up-pressure-vs-annas-archive-sci-hub-z-library-libgen-250203/), while U.S. authorities indicted its alleged operators.

This tension is the subject of [a paper](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13614533.2026.2742162) published earlier this month in the journal [New Review of Academic Librarianship](https://www.tandfonline.com/journals/racl20), written by two librarians at Nigerian universities.

*The paper*
[![the article](https://torrentfreak.com/images/articleheader.png)](https://torrentfreak.com/images/articleheader.png)

The peer-reviewed academic paper doesn’t pick a side and the lead author kindly shared a copy of their work with TorrentFreak. While the conclusions are not immediately newsworthy, there was one reference that stood out right away.

## A TorrentFreak Article That Doesn’t Exist

In a section on enforcement against Z-Library, the authors write that major academic publishers, including Elsevier and Springer Nature, have taken similar legal action. The reference is a 2021 TorrentFreak article, as shown below.

*The claim*

![The claim](https://torrentfreak.com/images/zlib-paper-claim.png)

The problem is that this particular legal action is new to us. The [reference section](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/ref/10.1080/13614533.2026.2742162?scroll=top) lists the article as “Publishers continue crackdown on shadow libraries” and links to the TorrentFreak homepage. However, we have never published an article with that title.

We did report that [Elsevier and Springer Nature obtained a UK blocking order](https://torrentfreak.com/?p=200675) against Sci-Hub, and that [several Z-Library domains](https://torrentfreak.com/?p=201455) were temporarily suspended over copyright infringement claims. However, we’re not aware of this particular legal action against Z-Library.

Looking more closely, we see more claims that don’t seem to fit. The paper’s account of the Z-Library criminal case, for example, states that the U.S. Department of Justice “arrested individuals” in 2022.

*Some references*

![references](https://torrentfreak.com/images/citations.png)

The DOJ’s [own press release](https://www.justice.gov/usao-edny/pr/two-russian-nationals-charged-running-massive-e-book-piracy-website) says that the two alleged operators, whose [indictment we covered](https://torrentfreak.com/u-s-indicts-two-russians-for-running-the-z-library-piracy-ring221117/) at the time, were arrested in Argentina “at the request of the United States.” The title the paper gives for that press release doesn’t exist either.

## Plausible but Inaccurate

These non-existent citations appear plausible, as they cite real publications and organizations in a fitting context. However, that doesn’t mean they are accurate. When we went over the full reference list, we noticed that this applies to more sources.

The paper cites a 2019 Harvard Journal of Law & Technology article by IP law professor Daniel Gervais, titled “The future of intellectual property in the shadow of shadow libraries.” The [table of contents](https://jolt.law.harvard.edu/assets/articlePDFs/v32/32HarvJLTechIntro2.pdf) of the cited issue doesn’t include it, and the cited pages are taken up by two articles on other topics.

The same applies to a 2019 College & Research Libraries article attributed to Carol Tenopir and colleagues. The [cited issue](https://crl.acrl.org/index.php/crl/issue/view/1120/showToc) has no article by Tenopir. She did co-author a 2019 article on a similar topic, but it appeared in a different journal, under a different title.

Other references that couldn’t be located include a 2019 article credited to Björn Brembs and articles credited to authors named Smith, Piper, Okike and Aronson, as well as a Kaspersky report on “the hidden dangers of shadow libraries.”

In total, we had trouble finding at least ten of the 44 references in the paper, as well as others that didn’t necessarily back the stated claims.

## 100% AI?

The obvious question is where these mystery references came from. The paper’s acknowledgements may offer a hint in the right direction, as they mention that AI was used in the research and writing process.

“We confirm that Consensus AI was used in sifting through large volume of literature and extract relevant information,” the authors write. They add that ChatGPT was used to improve the language of parts of the paper.

*Acknowledgements*

![Acknowledgements](https://torrentfreak.com/images/zlib-paper-acknowledgements.png)

AI chatbots are known to invent references, often with plausible titles credited to real experts in the field. This could explain the findings, although it doesn’t prove that AI wrote these references.

We also ran the paper’s [abstract](https://www.pangram.com/history/1dce5a18-e55a-476a-bfab-46c044c4a2b4) and [conclusion](https://www.pangram.com/history/8e236e20-0721-4773-85ca-4e13f0dc1c68?ucc=ZG2hDn2922Q&utm_source=pangram_share&utm_medium=copy_link) through the AI detection tool [Pangram](https://www.pangram.com/). For both sections, the tool reported that “100% of the text is likely AI-generated.” These types of tools are not perfect and can produce false positives, but it would fit the picture.

*Pangram’s conclusion*

![pangram says 100% AI](https://torrentfreak.com/images/pangramconclude.png)

It would be rather ironic if AI caused these errors. It would mean that a paper in a commercial, peer-reviewed journal, published by [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/), relied on AI to write about Z-Library. Meanwhile, many rightsholders are suing AI companies for using shadow libraries to train AI models.

Interestingly, the paper explicitly warns that sites such as Z-Library hurt publishers’ revenue, which funds peer review and editing.

“The widespread use of Z-Library poses significant challenges to the sustainability of academic publishing. Publishers rely on subscription fees and paywalls to fund the peer review, editing, and distribution of scholarly works,” it reads, ...
