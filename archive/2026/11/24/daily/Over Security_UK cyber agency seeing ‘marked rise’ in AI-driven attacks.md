# UK cyber agency seeing ‘marked rise’ in AI-driven attacks

Britain’s National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) said Tuesday it has observed “a marked rise” since September in cyberattacks assisted by artificial intelligence.

Echoing a message the agency has repeatedly stressed, the issue is not that AI is making established cybersecurity practices obsolete, but its adoption by attackers is reducing the margin for error among organizations that are failing to do the basics.

In a [blog post](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blogs/familiar-weaknesses-exploited-faster-defending-against-agentic-cyber-attacks), the NCSC warned the technology was increasing attackers’ speed, scale and persistence while also reducing their costs — largely due to the widespread availability of the hacking frameworks “combined with cheap, capable open models or illegal API resellers.”

“Organisations across multiple sectors are reporting incidents as a result of threat actors using ‘agentic offensive frameworks’,” said the NCSC, referring to the software “originally designed for red teaming being applied beyond its defensive purpose.”

The agency said it was “worth noting” that “none of the weaknesses being exploited are new or novel” and that the tools being used by these AI agents “provide no new capability the typical threat actor would have access to.”

The post — co-authored by David Chismon, the NCSC’s chief technology officer for architecture, alongside incident handler Kris B and lead data scientist Ellen T — identified Artex as the predominant framework receiving attention at the moment.

Artex was [reportedly](https://therecord.media/european-commission-confirms-cyberattack-using-ai) behind an incident compromising the European Commission last month, with the FBI [warning](https://therecord.media/artex-ai-tool-fbi-flash-alert) that it was being used both by criminals and state-sponsored groups.

Naming five other frameworks — Cairn, PentAGI, RedAmon, Xalgorix and Darkmoon — the NCSC said “different tools come and go,” but that the important lesson was to recognize how they all contributed to “an uplift in capability.”

Compared with human attackers without those tools, the agentic frameworks were credited with working more quickly, being exhaustive in their searches, instantly learning from their failures and being cheap to run, at scale.

The downside for attackers, and the positive for defenders, is that this activity is noisy – meaning the frameworks are easy to detect — although the NCSC warns this “may not always be the case if future frameworks prioritise stealth.”

## Getting ahead

Launching the agency’s annual review, NCSC chief executive Richard Horne [warned](https://therecord.media/uk-ncsc-predicts-rise-cyberattacks-coming-months) that the threat was increasing “not because AI has invented new ways to attack us,” but because weaknesses that organizations had previously tolerated could now be discovered and exploited at scale.

Chismon recently [warned](https://therecord.media/ai-set-to-help-attackers-more-than-defenders) that AI was likely to benefit attackers more than defenders, at least in the short term, as ideas about autonomous AI-powered defensive systems were not yet mature enough to be relied upon. The imbalance means AI-enabled cyberattacks are expected to grow as automated defenses struggle to keep pace.

“AI does not make established cyber security controls obsolete,” stated the blog post. “In fact building strong foundations has never mattered more. AI is making weaknesses easier to find and faster to exploit; a forgotten key or an unprotected API which might once have gone unnoticed for years can be found in minutes.”

“Nearly everything the NCSC has seen can be prevented by implementing cyber security best practice,” it added.

“This is your chance to get ahead of the threat. Consider how your organisation would answer the questions above, and make sure your answers hold up against an attacker that never needs a break and never gets tired of asking.”
