# Australia passes tough legislation requiring big tech to preempt foreseeable harms

Australia’s Parliament on Wednesday greenlit legislation creating sweeping online safety reforms that open the possibility of billions of dollars in fines for big tech companies that fail to preempt online harms before they occur.

Known as the Digital Duty of Care Act, the legislation sailed through Parliament with strong backing from Prime Minister Anthony Albanese.

His administration has been a global leader in fighting against online safety harms and spearheaded Australia’s pioneering strict age requirements for social media use that took effect in December 2025 and have since been [copied](https://therecord.media/spain-to-ban-social-media-children) by governments worldwide.

The pending law will mandate that online providers establish protocols to ensure Australians are protected from threats of violence and rape as well as behavior that could cause significant harms.

Platforms are required to detect and decrease risks from harmful content and must bolster protections for users under 18 against pornography, suicide and eating disorders. They also must undertake annual written risk assessments and call out major platform design changes such as endless scrolling that create new harms.

Under the law, users also will be able to opt out of algorithmic feeds and will be required to prove their age to access porn.

After the law passed, Albanese [reportedly](https://apnews.com/article/australia-digital-duty-care-law-tech-harm-b3f044575e9d8106f30a14eee2b89a81) told Parliament it will be a “powerful instrument to protect our children and our society from online harms by placing the onus on the tech platforms to ensure a safer online environment.”

“This is a victory for our children and young people. The dangers were unforeseen when the digital world was young,” Albanese said. “But now that the digital world has matured, they can be easily recognized and they can be remedied.”

Similar legislation is already in place in the [U.K.](https://therecord.media/online-safety-bill-uk-end-to-end-encryption) and [European Union](https://therecord.media/european-parliament-elections-interferance-big-tech-rules). A bill seeking to impose a duty of care against large platforms has been introduced by U.S. lawmakers but has [stagnated](https://therecord.media/kids-online-safety-act-congress) in Congress.

U.S. officials have accused the Australian legislation of violating free speech, threatening American companies’ business models and imposing vague requirements that will cause tech giants to overhaul their services to avoid liability.

“We are particularly concerned by potential mandates that would allow regulators to impose rigid, one-size-fits-all platform design requirements – such as forced chronological feeds or opt-in recommendation systems,” the U.S. Embassies and Consulates in Australia said in a September [public comment](https://au.usembassy.gov/u-s-government-response-to-the-australian-consultation-on-the-online-safety-amendment-digital-duty-of-care-bill-2026/).

The legislation does not undermine free speech, Albanese [told](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/digital-duty-care) Parliament in October, saying that “rather than someone in the United States deciding” what people see, now Australians can “decide themselves.”

The law will require platforms to “give us more choice over the content and the features that we use, including the feeds that we choose,” Albanese said.
