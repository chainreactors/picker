Imagine something as mission-critical as the brakes on a trailer being vulnerable to unauthorized control. One Australian manufacturer made the interesting choice of building Bluetooth into a trailer brake controller without adequately securing its critical braking functions.

Anyone within Bluetooth range of an unpatched Elecbrakes trailer controller could potentially trigger its brakes or apply more braking force than intended, without needing to authenticate.

The discovery comes courtesy of a pair of security researchers from Element Digital, Patrick Forck and Jeremy McCarthy. Their investigation began earlier this year when Forck was driving down the road and noticed that his physical Elecbrakes In-Car Remote had connected to someone else's trailer without pairing or prompting him.

REG AD

“It had connected mid-drive, without pairing or a prompt, to a brake controller that wasn’t mine, on a trailer I’d never seen, belonging to a driver who had no idea,” the researchers [wrote](https://elementdigital.com.au/blog/brake-check-trailer-brakes-over-bluetooth/). That one-off connection triggered a full-scale investigation by the pair.

REG AD

For those unfamiliar with trailer brake controllers, these devices are commonly used on larger trailers. Australian manufacturer Elecbrakes sells such controllers in Australia and New Zealand. Depending on jurisdiction, trailers over a certain weight are required to have their own braking system, which Elecbrakes manufactures. Elecbrakes offers two versions that are designed to be easier to install than traditional controllers: One that’s mounted in a vehicle, and one that is mounted directly on a trailer. That trailer-mounted unit can receive Bluetooth commands from the Elecbrakes smartphone app or a separate in-car remote.

Given the importance of such control units to road safety, it’s easy to imagine how serious such a vulnerability could be if it proved to be easy to exploit, and boy, did it.

Forck and McCarthy admitted they’re infrastructure security guys, not software devs, so they turned to Claude Code to dissect a decompiled copy of the Elecbrakes app, which Claude was happy to oblige.

“What came back from the analysis was a map of the device’s entire Bluetooth GATT profile: 47 characteristics across four services, commands sent as plain unencrypted byte arrays, no checksums, no sequence numbers,” the researchers noted. To make matters worse, there was no authentication required for critical braking commands, even though other Bluetooth functions required pairing.
