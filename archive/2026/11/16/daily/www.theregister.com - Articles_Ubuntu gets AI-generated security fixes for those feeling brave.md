Canonical is offering Ubuntu Pro users an opt-in channel for AI-generated security patches, deploying bots to help tackle the growing pile of vulnerabilities other bots are uncovering.

Canonical's VP for "Excellence Engineering," Pierre Guillemin, gave the second keynote at the recent Ubuntu Summit, introducing the [Edge archive](https://canonical.com/blog/new-edge-archive-offers-opt-in-ai-driven-security-remediation-and-backports), an optional repository of security fixes generated by the company's Surge framework.

As the announcement describes it: "We built Surge, a multi-agent orchestration framework for CVE remediation and backporting, and have reached a high level of confidence in its capabilities. We will not automatically upload Surge-generated fixes to our stable releases, but from today they are available to Ubuntu Pro users in the new Edge archive."

REG AD

Edge offers patches before extended testing and qualification are complete, allowing users to assess them themselves. Canonical says updates delivered through the normal security channels will remain human-validated.

REG AD

Ubuntu Summit now runs twice a year. Last week's [Ubuntu Summit 26.10](https://discourse.ubuntu.com/t/ubuntu-summit-26-10-timetable/90216) followed a rather unfortunately timed [server outage for the company](https://www.theregister.com/os-platforms/2026/11/11/canonical-web-services-get-stage-fright-ahead-of-ubuntu-summit/5309186).

At the [previous such event in June](https://www.theregister.com/software/2026/06/08/canonical-sends-ubuntu-into-the-ai-agent-era/5252373), the company announced a move into agentic AI, but this new one is bolder still. Guillemin noted that "October 2026 saw almost as many CVEs as all of 2025." Surge is a "multi-agent orchestration framework" to remediate vulnerabilities in Ubuntu's userspace. In other words, not the kernel, but even so, it is ambitious.

The idea is to use a group of different LLMs to triage new vulnerabilities, find existing fixes, use multiple competing models to backport them to the versions in Ubuntu, and run automatic testing on them. Then a different group of "judging models" will evaluate the fixes and look for consensus. When candidates are chosen, they pass through normal Ubuntu package testing. If there are edge cases, low confidence scores, deadlocks, or other problems, then the process will be escalated to the staff of Canonical's security team.
