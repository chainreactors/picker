# Introducing Our New Surveillance Self-Defense Guide: Online Privacy for Parents and Youth

![](https://www.eff.org/files/styles/resized_banner/public/banner_library/og-ssd-logo.png?itok=H_ZsdBX6)

The internet is a vast and confusing place no matter your age. Trying to help youth engage with it safely and privately is a complicated topic that touches on many different skillsets and ideas. But just because it’s complicated doesn’t mean it’s impossible. We’ve launched our [Online Privacy for Parents and Youth](https://ssd.eff.org/module/online-privacy-for-parents-and-youth) Surveillance Self-Defense guide in hopes to provide a framework for finding solutions that work for you.

EFF rolls out this crucial resource as state and local governments consider [ill-advised laws](https://www.eff.org/issues/age-verification) that cut young people off from essential information and experiences. This particularly harms vulnerable youth and marginalized groups who often find safety in supportive online communities they can't access offline. Such laws also entail age gating, which compromises the privacy of all internet users and stifles young users’ ability to [learn, grow](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/12/lawmakers-must-listen-young-people-regulating-their-internet-access-2025-review), and [express themselves](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/06/eff-court-young-people-have-first-amendment-rights).

Young people have unique needs and those needs change over time. Trying to protect from everything all the time is an exhausting process, but the security community has long had a solution for this: [security planning](https://ssd.eff.org/module/your-security-plan) (also called threat modeling). The idea is to provide a series of questions designed to help you focus on what’s truly important. When it comes to online privacy, this is as applicable for youth and parents as it is for anyone else.

We used a security plan as our foundation for this guide because deciding what is and isn’t important is the first step we should all take when assessing our privacy risks. But this isn’t a place for quick fixes, and every young person has their unique needs and considerations. There is no single tool or technology fix that can substitute for spending time learning, and then teaching a young person, how the internet works, how to use it, what sorts of harms exist, and how to navigate those harms.

Solutions are rarely about the tools or software you use. It’s about understanding the threats and concerns you have, and then determining what steps you can take to mitigate those risks. We hope our [Online Privacy for Parents and Youth](https://ssd.eff.org/module/online-privacy-for-parents-and-youth) guide can help provide the outline to get the conversation started.
