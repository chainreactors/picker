### About "model wellbeing" and simulated minds

In the public discourse on AI, a term that crops up repeatedly is "model wellbeing" -- in essence, should we treat a model like a living being, and take care that it is treated well.

This post is to clarify my thoughts about this.

Please consider two thought experiments: The "weather forecast" experiment and the "characters in a novel" experiment.

### The "weather forecast" thought experiment

Let's imagine I have a source of near-infinite computational power, and I would like to produce the most accurate weather forecast imaginable. So I shrink my simulation grid, and eventually I start simulating forest canopies, animal methane emissions, and even human populations. As I get more and more detailed in my simulation, I start simulating individual human behavior, imbuing each simulated human with various emotional and mental states and motivations. My weather forecast becomes a sort of world simulator, with an astonishing level of detail, and indeed, I succeed in doing economic forecasts on it that greatly exceed the forecasting I could've done in the real world.

So in this thought experiment, we have created a simulated earth.

Do we need to treat the simulated beings in this simulated earth like human beings? Is it unethical to test things like "what if simulated Europe or North America would be hit by simultaneous earthquakes" or "how about a pandemic"?

### The "characters in a novel" thought experiment

When I read a novel, I can imagine the characters quite vividly, and if the novel is well-written, most readers can simulate the behavior or the inner monologue of a character in situations that the novel did not foresee. Our brains perform a sort of computation, and "simulate" characters from the novel. When fully immersed in a role-playing game, humans do this sort of "simulation" for entertainment.

What are the ethical implications of treating characters in a novel poorly? You are treating them poorly in a simulation, just one which happens to run on biological substrate. Are you allowed to kill fictional characters in your imagination? Are you allowed to quit thinking about fictional characters, or is this akin to death for them?

Children's books have been written about this, and my son had an imaginary friend as a toddler. Clearly, his brain simulated another being. Was he in the wrong to quit thinking about the imaginary friend?

### LLMs are simulations of an inner monologue

When we discuss what rights to bestow on models, we need to be clear that an LLM is **a simulation of an inner monologue**. It is in the same way a living being that imaginary characters in a novel are, and it is just as alive as the characters in the game of Lemmings were. We can discuss whether there is a line at which "ability to solve human-posed problems" bestows special rights, but we generally do not treat more intelligent animals vastly nicer than we treat other animals, and I personally would always grant the simplest animals more rights than a simulated character in my imagination.

### Perhaps I am a "carbonist" or "anti-simulationist"?

You can raise the objection that I am clearly biased toward carbon-based life as we know it, because I do not think that simulated minds - be it my own brain simulating them, or a cluster of dozens or hundreds of GPUs - have the same rights as a carbon-based life form.

What I do ask though is: If you're in favor of treating models as if they were some form of life, and if you worry about model well-being, why do you provide special status to minds simulated by a GPU vs. minds simulated by another human imagining them?
