eMudhra's private key exposure has been a security disaster, but it also means that you can block those annoying ads on smart TVs if you’re so inclined.

Luckily for those with some know-how and a hatred of smart TV ads, Czech software developer Daniel Vrátil has done the work for you and published his project [on GitHub](https://github.com/danvratil/samsung-tv-adspoofer) for anyone who wants to take a stab at it - and do the legwork to add support for their own ad-riddled television.

Vrátil [said](https://dvratil.cz/2026/11/hijacking-samsung-tv-smart-hub-ads/) he’d been blocking ads on his Samsung smart TV using a Pi-hole (DNS-based ad-blocking software that can run on a Raspberry Pi, for those unfamiliar), but right around the time the eMudhra certificate leak mess unfolded, he had messed something up while tinkering with his home lab and the ads were back.

REG AD

First, here's a bit of background on the eMudhra mess. The company, based in India, operated a publicly trusted HTTPS certificate authority and says it has issued more than 60 million digital certificates. As we learned [last month](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/10/20/indian-certification-authority-emudhras-private-keys-compromised-one-of-them-was-the-number-4/5304312), eMudhra wasn't doing its best to keep its cryptographic keys safe: Eight intermediate CA private keys were found to be absurdly weak, with one being the number four and the others consisting of two- or three-digit numbers that could be guessed with little effort.

REG AD

eMudhra has since [left the CA racket](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/10/22/disgraced-indian-cert-issuer-leaves-the-cert-issuing-business/5304848), saying that it wasn’t a big deal to it since issuing certs was a small part of its business. The company has blamed a [bad software script](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/11/12/disgraced-former-ca-emudhra-blames-buggy-script-for-hopelessly-weak-certificates/5309244) put in place to generate keys that its hardware couldn’t handle as the root of the issue.

What all that means, in a practical sense, is that anyone could forge an eMudhra certificate to spoof a website and do something malicious. It also means that security researchers who were able to get their hands on devices that still trust eMudhra certificates could have a field day learning how their communications work, which is exactly what Vrátil took advantage of....
