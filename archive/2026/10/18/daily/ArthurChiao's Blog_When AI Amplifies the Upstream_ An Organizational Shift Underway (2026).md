# 1 Introduction

AI is changing not only how software gets built, but how direction travels
through a company. Discussion often focuses on faster execution. A less discussed
change is happening upstream: a passing intuition can now become a detailed
proposal within minutes, complete with architecture diagrams, milestones,
interfaces, and risk lists.

When its author has agenda-setting power, that output may arrive downstream not
as one candidate among many, but as a direction to review and execute. This is
not because managers have suddenly become arbitrary or technically incompetent.
Top-down direction existed long before AI. What changed is the practical limit
on how quickly the upstream can turn ideas into apparently finished plans. The
downstream’s capacity to test assumptions, reconcile dependencies, and carry
consequences remains finite.

One side can therefore generate directions at near-zero marginal cost; the other
must spend scarce professional attention digesting, falsifying, and making them
work. The danger is not merely more work. **Processing instructions
begins to displace exercising judgment.**

# 2 A Mirror: AI-Generated Mathematics

In October 2026, OpenAI published 719 mathematical manuscripts generated by its
internal models, grouped into 372 result families, and framed the release as
“sharing AI’s progress in mathematics”. The controversy was not only whether
the results were true. It was also who would verify, understand, and explain
them: a finite human mathematical community. Some mathematicians objected that
checking this volume of unsolicited output would turn them into unpaid labor
confirming or refuting OpenAI’s claims of progress.

The episode exposes one structure with unusual clarity:
when an upstream can produce apparently finished results cheaply and
at scale, while verification and integration still depend on scarce human
attention, the output itself can become a burden on the
downstream.

Mathematical proof and product delivery are not the same, but both reveal a
**`generation–verification asymmetry`**:
one side cheaply creates candidates for
attention; the other spends expert time deciding which deserve to be taken
seriously and turning answers into shared knowledge.

Inside a company, the asymmetry is more consequential. A mathematical community
can publicly decline to validate a claim or reject the agenda imposed on it.
Employees receiving a proposal through a chain of authority usually cannot.
They must investigate it, explain why it will or will not work, and often execute
it while remaining accountable for the result.

# 3 When the Upstream Gains Near-Infinite Expressive Power

## 3.1 From Intuition to Apparently Complete Plan

Organizations have always had an upstream and a downstream. Managers set goals,
resources, and priorities; front-line and middle-layer teams translated them
into workable plans using local knowledge. The process was often slow and
bureaucratic, but it gave reality a chance to enter before a direction hardened
into an instruction.

AI weakens that boundary. An intuition can be packaged as a coherent plan
without first passing through people who know the systems, dependencies, and
history involved. The plan may be useful. The structural temptation comes from
its appearance: when polished output is cheap, it is easy to assume that problem
definition, constraint discovery, and tradeoff analysis have already been done.
Anyone who objects must then explain why a complete-looking proposal will not
survive contact with reality — and overcome both its polish and the authority
behind it.

## 3.2 The Upstream Is Not One Person

In a medium-sized or large enterprise, the upstream is rarely a single leader.
AI gives many people the ability to inject detailed demands into the same
execution system at once.

Requests used to be partly filtered by the effort required to formulate and
coordinate them. As those filters weaken, requests arrive already packaged as
plausible plans, but may never have been merged, prioritized, or made mutually
consistent. Each can look reasonable in isolation while the same downstream
teams must reconcile all of them in practice. The result is a
**demand explosion from uncoordinated sources**, all
drawing on the same finite capacity for attention, judgment, and execution.

## 3.3 Generation Is Visible; Digestion Is Invisible

The upstream generates plans. The downstream must assess them, reconcile them
with existing commitments, fill in omissions, resolve conflicts, and own the
consequences after launch.

Generation is visible and easy to count: proposals written, tasks created,
decisions announced. Digestion is mostly invisible: tracing dependencies,
discovering conflicting directions, explaining why a reasonable-looking plan is
not feasible, or repairing the gaps between an instruction and reality. The
proposal gets credited; the work required to make it real — or prove that it
should not be made real — is mistaken for routine execution.

# 4 From Professional Judgment to Instruction Processing

## 4.1 Judgment Is Learned Through Participation

Professional judgment develops through a repeated loop: forming a view, testing
it against reality, receiving feedback, and revising it. In a functioning
division of labor, execution teams helped define problems, surface constraints,
weigh tradeoffs, propose alternatives, and see the consequences of decisions.
That participation gave people room not only to use judgment, but to develop it.

When more of the solution arrives from above already specified, the execution
team remains busy, but its work changes. It spends less time exploring the
problem and shaping a solution, and more time interpreting, reconciling, and
working around instructions.
