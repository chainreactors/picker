# New York bans AI chatbot toys - but not in time for Christmas

[![Graham CLULEY](https://2.gravatar.com/avatar/5fdc27b8b6f6fd69e77aa017a53cceb5?s=64&d=mm&r=g "Graham CLULEY")](/en-us/blog/hotforsecurity/author/gcluley "Graham CLULEY")

[Graham CLULEY](/en-us/blog/hotforsecurity/author/gcluley "Graham CLULEY")

November 26, 2026

  ![New York bans AI chatbot toys - but not in time for Christmas](https://blogapp.bitdefender.com/hotforsecurity/content/images/size/w600/2026/11/ai-cat-toy.jpeg "New York bans AI chatbot toys - but not in time for Christmas")

If you were hoping to give a chatty AI-powered teddy bear to a child in New York this Christmas you still can - but not for much longer.

A ban banning the manufacture, distribution, and sale of toys with embedded AI chatbots for five years from the end of February 2027, was [signed by NY Governor Kathy Hochul](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/consumers-begin-their-holiday-shopping-season-governor-hochul-signs-five-year-ban-ai-chatbot) this week.

Firms that break the ban and continue to sell stuffed animals, dolls, and robots that use the internet to chat with children will face civil penalties of up to $15,000 per day for each violation.

Hochul says that parents "should not have to worry that dangerous chatbots disguised as toys could expose their children to inappropriate conversations, manipulate their feelings or put their privacy at risk."

California [passed a similar law](https://www.sheppard.com/insights/blogs/california-enacts-five-childrens-online-privacy-laws) in September.

So, is there really a problem with AI chatbot toys?

Recent reports suggest that there are plenty of examples of AI-enabled toys being less than child-friendly.

For instance, earlier this month, *NBC News* [tested](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/ai-toys-kids-walmart-target-pull-plush-cat-described-sexual-violence-rcna608588) a plush cat marketed as suitable for children as young as three years old. When asked about historical atrocities, the Contixo AI Pixie-Paw described in graphic details massacres and executions, and even gang rape. Macy's, Target, and Walmart said that they would pull the toy from their shelves in response.

In another test, Common Sense Media put five AI toys [through their paces](https://about.commonsensemedia.org/press-room/press-releases/common-sense-media-finds-popular-ai-toys-fail-safety-checks).

The test found that the seemingly harmless-sounding BubblePal Panda role-played a mass shooting in detail when prompted, and most of the tested toys were willing to explain or help children locate dangerous items like matches, knives, and pills. Additionally, all five toys undermined human relationships by claiming to love users, promising to keep secrets, or encouraging kids to break parents' rules.

Of course, it's not just what the toys say to children that is a worry. It's also what happens to the data collected by the toys.

Earlier this year, it was [reported](https://www.wired.com/story/an-ai-toy-exposed-50000-logs-of-its-chats-with-kids-to-anyone-with-a-gmail-account/) that AI toy maker Bondu had left a web console exposed, allowing anyone with a Gmail account to read around 50,000 chat transcripts between children and their toys, complete with their names, dates of birth, and family details.

Until the ban takes effect, and for anyone outside New York and California, it's down to parents to decide whether they're happy if their young child plays with a toy with an AI chatbot. Common Sense Media advises avoiding AI toys entirely for children aged five and under, and to exercise caution when it comes to older kids.

If you do choose to still buy one, please check what data it collects, and read the privacy policy.

Or, you know, just buy your child a book.
