After embarrassing reports revealed weak private keys, one of which was just a single digit, Indian certification authority eMudhra has decided to take the L and get out of the global SSL/TLS cert game entirely.

eMudhra [announced](https://emudhra.com/en/press-release/emudhra-to-exit-global-ssl-tls-certificate-business) Thursday that it was abandoning that arm of its business. Sure, the intense scrutiny heaped on the company was a factor, but the firm also argued that growing compliance costs and competition from free certificate providers had made the business increasingly difficult to justify.

“Global SSL/TLS is a small part of eMudhra’s business contributing to about 1 percent of the global revenue,” the company explained, noting that free SSL/TLS cert issuance from large firms and increased compliance and data sovereignty requirements have made it a minor part of the firm’s operation, and one that doesn’t merit so much bad press.

REG AD

“The level of social media amplification around this incident has brought scrutiny that is out of proportion to SSL/TLS’s place in our business,” eMudhra explained. “In view of this, after detailed deliberations our board has concluded that withdrawing from global SSL/TLS is the right decision for eMudhra and its shareholders.”

REG AD

An argument could be made that, even if SSL/TLS accounts for just 1 percent of its global revenue, a private CA key with a value of 4 still deserves a thorough public shaming.

It’s only been two days since eMudhra’s weak private keys were [exposed in public reports](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/10/20/indian-certification-authority-emudhras-private-keys-compromised-one-of-them-was-the-number-4/5304312), following a report to Mozilla on October 17, and it wasn’t exactly hard for security researchers to recover them: The eight affected private-key values ranged from the number 4 (yes – a plain old number 4), to 56 and 66, to the far more complicated 115, 155, 156, 230, and 237.

No quantum computer required to crack those keys. Wow – better [save the qubits](https://www.theregister.com/special-features/2023/04/26/worried-about-quantum-breaking-encryption-give-it-30-years/826241).

This wasn’t just an embarrassing collection of numbers, either: The weak keys belonged to eight intermediate certificate authorities, potentially allowing attackers to forge certificates trusted by systems that hadn’t blocked them. eMudhra, however, maintains that the affected CAs had issued no end-entity certificates.
