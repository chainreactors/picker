# n0n extortion gang leaks data of JPMorgan Chase, Visa, and Mastercard customers

[![Graham CLULEY](https://2.gravatar.com/avatar/5fdc27b8b6f6fd69e77aa017a53cceb5?s=64&d=mm&r=g "Graham CLULEY")](/en-us/blog/hotforsecurity/author/gcluley "Graham CLULEY")

[Graham CLULEY](/en-us/blog/hotforsecurity/author/gcluley "Graham CLULEY")

November 20, 2026

  ![n0n extortion gang leaks data of JPMorgan Chase, Visa, and Mastercard customers](https://blogapp.bitdefender.com/hotforsecurity/content/images/size/w600/2026/11/non-cards.jpeg "n0n extortion gang leaks data of JPMorgan Chase, Visa, and Mastercard customers")

Data belonging to customers of three of the biggest companies in global finance has been leaked online, after hackers breached third-party service providers working for JPMorgan Chase, Visa, and Mastercard.

The finance companies have all acknowledged that security incidents have occurred at unnamed third--party companies, after the n0n extortion gang named all three on its dark web leak site earlier this week.

In JPMorgan Chase's case, it [tweeted](https://x.com/jpmorgan/status/2123408240104853510) that although it believed "the attackers accessed certain marketing data held by those vendors, which may include some customer names and email addresses" there had been "no disruption" to its services.

![](https://blogapp.bitdefender.com/hotforsecurity/content/images/2026/11/jpmorgan-tweet.jpeg)

Mastercard also said that its payment network had not been affected, and that cardholders could continue to use their cards.

According to Visa, the breach occurred at an unnamed third-party provider based in Asia Pacific.

n0n claims to have stolen "hundreds of thousands of campaign interactions" related to JPMorgan Chase. All three of the corporations were given 48 hours to negotiate with the extortion gang, but none of them paid. So, n0n published data on its website exposing the details of thousands of customers.

![](https://blogapp.bitdefender.com/hotforsecurity/content/images/2026/11/leak-site.jpeg)

News of the security breach impacted the share prices of all three companies, after a jittery week of high-profile technology outages in the United States.

According to [*Reuters*](https://www.reuters.com/legal/government/jpmorgan-says-third-party-vendors-targeted-cyberattack-2026-11-19/), the leaked JPMorgan Chase data included more than 16,000 customer email addresses, and what appeared to be the email addresses of some 124 employees of the bank. Some of the customer information included phone numbers, and post codes.

Clearly scammers and fraudsters could exploit information like that in a variety of ways, including by contacting members of the public claiming to be employees of their bank.

Meanwhile, according to a *Cybernews* [report](https://cybernews.com/security/visa-jpmorgan-data-breach/), the leaked Visa data includes details of over 6000 premium card holders who are signed up to Asia-Pacific dining programmes, including names, phone numbers, the messaging apps they use, and restaurant booking details.

n0n is believed to be a Russia-based extortion gang, and despite only having been operating for a couple of months has already managed to claim the scalps of approximately 200 corporate victims.

Security researchers say n0n has typically broken into organisations using valid login credentials, harvested by info-stealing malware or obtained by credential-stuffing VPNs and poorly secured remote desktop (RDP) services, before using legitimate admin tools to gather data. It is not yet known how the vendors in this case were compromised.

Of course, financial institutions are very experienced at securing their own infrastructure. Where they appear to have fallen down on this occasion is through the use of third-party marketing agencies or loyalty scheme operators who have been less diligent in securing their systems.

You are only as safe as the weakest link in the chain. And customers don't really care whose server it was that leaked their data, when they entrusted it to your care in the first place.

The advice for concerned Chase, Visa or Mastercard customers is simple: be wary of unexpected emails, texts or calls claiming to come from your bank. It would not be a surprise at all if scammers exploit the breach by mentioning it to customers, and offering compensation, or inviting members of the public to "verify" their account.

If you are unsure, contact your bank directly using its published number.
