# U.S. Supreme Court Deals Final Blow to Corporate Human Rights Accountability

![](https://www.eff.org/files/styles/resized_banner/public/banner_library/china-eye.png?itok=VNzPC-s1)

For the past two decades, the U.S. Supreme Court has been slowly [weakening the Alien Tort Statute (ATS)](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2021/07/supreme-court-narrows-ability-hold-us-corporations-accountable-facilitating-human), a key human rights law that allows foreign nationals to sue persons or entities in civil court for harms resulting from violations of [international law](https://www.justsecurity.org/46352/original-meaning-alien-tort-statute/). The court’s [recent ruling](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-856_kjfm.pdf) in [*Cisco Systems, Inc. v. Doe I*](https://www.eff.org/cases/doe-i-v-cisco) dealt the law a final blow, foreclosing—absent new legislation by Congress—any future ability by plaintiffs to use the law to hold technology companies accountable for aiding and abetting human rights abuses. EFF [filed multiple amicus briefs in the case](https://www.eff.org/press/releases/us-tech-companies-must-be-accountable-us-courts-facilitating-persecution-and-torture), including most recently in the Supreme Court.

#### **The Supreme Court Rejected New Causes of Action Under the ATS**

The ATS was passed by the very first Congress in 1789 and in [just a few words](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R44947) gives U.S. federal courts jurisdiction to hear civil claims brought by foreign nationals against defendants who violated “the law of nations or a treaty of the United States." There has been a longstanding question about what counts as a valid civil claim under “the law of nations.”

In [*Sosa v. Alvarez-Machain*](https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=6103279683071618777) (2004), the Supreme Court had noted three historical international law claims that the First Congress likely had in mind: violation of [safe conducts](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law%3Aepil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e396?d=%2F10.1093%2Flaw%3Aepil%2F9780199231690%2Flaw-9780199231690-e396&p=emailA%2FjrmZXu%2FGx%2F6), infringement of the rights of ambassadors, and piracy, collectively known as the [Blackstone Three](https://tlblog.org/supreme-court-closes-the-door-on-the-alien-tort-statute/).

The *Sosa* court also held that federal courts may recognize *other* substantive causes of action under the ATS if they reflect international norms that are “specific, universal, and obligatory,” stating that this “door is still ajar subject to vigilant doorkeeping, and thus open to a narrow class of international norms today.”

Thus, the *Sosa* court recognized that the ATS could be used to bring claims related to historical violations of international law as well as select newer claims reflecting modern concepts of human rights.
