# UK leaves client-side scanning out of new child safety rules

Britain’s online safety regulator Ofcom said Monday it does not have the power to make technology companies scan people’s personal devices for child abuse images, a major statement amid the ongoing controversy about abusers’ use of commodity encrypted messaging platforms.

Ofcom made the [announcement](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/online-safety-additional-safety-measures) while finalizing new rules under the [Online Safety Act](https://therecord.media/online-safety-bill-uk-end-to-end-encryption). The regulator said it “does not have powers under the Act to regulate devices” in response to campaigners calling for the scanning technology while expressing concern child abusers were using encrypted services to escape detection.

Under the finalized rules, large technology platforms will have to use tools to proactively detect child sexual abuse material (CSAM) — even when that material has never been seen before and has not been digitally fingerprinted— but only for content that users share publicly.

For encrypted chats, Ofcom’s revised guidance allows the platforms to judge a whole group based on its profile picture, description, or coded terms backed by a British police report. The regulator acknowledged this kind of enforcement “may interfere significantly with users’ privacy.”

Under the rules set out Monday, which would take effect in February 2027 if approved by Parliament, all platforms that host user-shared content must permanently ban adults who are discovered to be sharing CSAM or to be grooming children.

There will be exceptions for bans, including for victims and potentially for those who promptly report unwanted images, designed to address the risk that CSAM could be shared to harass its recipients. Suspected terrorist, hate, suicide and self-harm content must also be kept out of children's recommendation feeds.

The regulator also said it would not for now be setting limits on how other platform users can comment, send gifts, or record children’s livestreams as the government’s proposed ban on under-16s using social media made those protections unnecessary.

Client-side scanning, a mechanism of running those tools on users’ own phones or computers before the messages are encrypted and sent, is a controversial topic.

Supporters argue it is the only way to find CSAM exchanged over end-to-end encrypted services, while security researchers and privacy campaigns argue scanning on device breaks the promise of encryption.

Apple explored the approach in 2021 when it announced a client-side scanning system that would check content on-device to see if it depicted child abuse. Following criticisms, Apple quietly shelved the plan in 2022.

Ofcom did not rule out any introduction of client-side scanning in the future, but instead left the issue to a different part of the Online Safety Act. Section 121 of the law allows the regulator to issue a “technology notice” ordering a company to use accredited software to find CSAM, including in private messages.

Under the law, before Ofcom can issue such a notice the technology must be accredited against accuracy standards set by the government. Those standards only came into force in September, and Ofcom has not announced that any tool has yet been accredited.

The regulator said its rules would not apply in cases where they would “materially compromise the security” of a service, but it warned that mere high costs — or the need for design changes — would not be sufficient defenses on their own.

Ofcom also dropped plans, which had been proposed alongside the CSAM measures in June 2025, that would have required platforms to detect fraud automatically. It said it had “a high degree of confidence” only in detection tools for CSAM images, and that it would consult on how to tackle other harms next year.
