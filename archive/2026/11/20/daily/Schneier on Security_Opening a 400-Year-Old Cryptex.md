Technically it’s not the fault of the cryptex mechanism.

If you watch carefully you see the pick-shim tool is pushed beyond the cryptex mechanism to the hasp mechanics which is where the design weakness is in that lock type design.

You can make a “one time use” pick-shim to do that job with a strip cut from the side wall of a soft drinks can with a swiss army knife[1] scissors. And it works as well on bypassing other mechanisms not just cryptex or similar combination mechanisms. The reason is “mechanical slop” that is “designed in for reliability of functionality”. Low manufacturing cost and large temperature range of operation and other “environment in use factors” needs a gap that on cheap locks is usually twice or more the thickness of the drink can wall.

Master Locks for instance have a whole range of “popular padlocks” where you can bypass the lock mechanism and activate the hasp mechanism.

As I’ve said a few times before on this blog the failing of most locks is “mechanical slop” enabling you to either “feel the unlock positions of pins, leavers, and wheels” or just bypass to the “lock bar or similar” that holds the bolt or hasp in the locked position.

There is often a simple mechanical interlock that could be “designed in” to reduce or stop “mechanical slop” issues working for lock-picking[2]. But it adds to manufacturing cost and arguably reduces reliability thus it does nor get done for “shareholder value” reasons (call it just another part of the more general “Corey Doctorow word” of the world by greed above all else of neo-con corporatism.

By the way as @Trevor notes the number of combinations on most combination locks is low often just a thousand or less[3]. Combined with the issue of “Mechanical slop” you can “feel them in” with only your fingers, thus cycle, briefcase, luggage, and padlocks having combination mechanisms can with practice be opened within a few minutes.

Another set of failings arise due to the way the combination is changed. On the more modern cryptex type locks it needs the indicator ring and notch plate to be linked mechanically but also have a hold in place mechanism. Often you can find the position the notch plate is set at with little more than a pin used for haberdashery / sewing[5]. You just push it in the side of the wheel and turn it till you “feel the notch” or other mechanism indicator.

A simple chrome steel pin with a largish head and weak push out spring can form an interlock that the external magnetic field “pulls in” to act as a second lock mechanism.
