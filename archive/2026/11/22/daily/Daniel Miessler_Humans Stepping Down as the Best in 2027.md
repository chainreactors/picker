# Humans Stepping Down as the Best in 2027

Computers are getting better than us at the work we're proudest of, and I'm okay with that

November 22, 2026

by Daniel Miessler

[#ai](/archives/?tag=ai) [#future](/archives/?tag=future) [#philosophy](/archives/?tag=philosophy) [#society](/archives/?tag=society)

[**AIL***1*](/blog/ai-influence-level-ail "AIL 1 — Human Created, Minor AI Assistance")

 Krahing…

[![A purple machine stands alone on the top block of a stone podium while a guitarist steps down past the medal he left behind, and two people laugh over a chess game on the lowest block](/images/humans-stepping-down-2027.webp)](/images/humans-stepping-down-2027.webp)

**I’m realizing that 2027 is going to be the year of humans stepping down as the best in things we value as humans.**

Programmers were first. Everybody knew that they were special and elite. And now, computers are better at programming.

In my world, it’s happening to us as security testers.

It seems to have just happened to mathematicians.

And now it’s happening to musicians as well.

> [Loading tweet...](https://twitter.com/mvanhorn/status/2124600373713498264)

## Why we value scarcity [​](#why-we-value-scarcity)

There’s something profound about this in multiple ways.

The first thing it makes me question is **why we associate value with scarcity**. I know the answer is evolution, but it doesn’t mean we need to follow evolution’s rules forever, and it feels like this is a good time to stop.

Like, why did we value programmers so highly? Because there were so few of them who could do that job and do it so well. It’s the classic evolutionary game of valuing the thing that only very few people have.

Well, I say fuck that.

It should not take away from human creation of music or art or applications or math just because computers can do it better.

Chess is bigger than ever, and a free iPhone app can beat [Magnus Carlsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnus_Carlsen) 100 out of 100 times they play. We still enjoyed getting good at chess, and we still enjoy playing other humans at it.

♟️ Deep Blue beat world champion Garry Kasparov in 1997. People kept playing chess anyway.

Or let’s take it further and say that computers are even better at making art, or something we truly cherish, like we’ve been talking about. So what?

I enjoy finding vulnerabilities in applications, even though I know [Sam Curry](https://samcurry.net/) exists. And if you spend some time with this application, he would probably find even cooler stuff.

🛠️ Sam Curry is the researcher who showed he could remotely control Kia and Subaru cars through their web apps.

## Where the loss actually comes from [​](#where-the-loss-actually-comes-from)

I think the distinction is what part you are getting enjoyment from.

I think in many cases we are conflating different sources of the feeling of loss. It’s true that it can be disheartening to see AI do work that would’ve taken us days or weeks, and it does it in a few minutes, and it’s even better than we would’ve done it. But calculators are already like that in arithmetic, and it doesn’t bother us at all.

🧮 Nobody feels diminished that a calculator does long division faster than they can.

**I think the question is which part of the work we attach our identity to.**

Is it the fact that we are elite in our field and are therefore respected as being elite?

Is it the amount of time that we spent on the project?

What is the output that we ultimately create?

I think it’s obviously some combination of these, but we’re gonna have to do some deep thinking on this as more and more of our intellectual pillars fall to AI.

I think similar to so many things in life, this is going to depend greatly on how we look at it. And how we adjust from there.

## Summary [​](#summary)

1. Programmers went first, and security testers, mathematicians and musicians are right behind them
2. We valued programmers partly because there were so few of them, which is an old evolutionary habit
3. A free phone app beats Magnus Carlsen every time, and people still love playing chess
4. A lot of the loss we feel comes down to which part of the work holds our identity
5. How much this hurts depends a lot on how we choose to look at it
