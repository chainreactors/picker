# UK court confirms British government served Apple with secret encryption notice

The British government served Apple with a secret legal notice targeting the company’s cryptographic protections for iCloud accounts, a tribunal has confirmed, ending almost two years of official refusal to acknowledge what had been widely reported.

In a [judgment](https://investigatorypowerstribunal.org.uk/judgement/1-mr-vincent-kearney-2-british-broadcasting-corporation-v-1-chief-constable-of-the-police-service-of-northern-ireland-2-chief-constable-of-durham-constabulary-3-security-service-4-governme/) handed down Thursday, the Investigatory Powers Tribunal — the only court in the country that can hear certain national security cases — rejected the government’s “neither confirm nor deny” policy based on the argument that even confirming the existence of the notice would damage national security.

The court’s three judges stated explicitly that the government “has given Apple a technical capability notice,” referencing the legal power which the government used to demand that Apple retain the ability to access the content of iCloud accounts.

At the core of the dispute is Apple’s Advanced Data Protection (ADP) feature which generates and stores encryption keys on users’ own devices instead of Apple’s servers. Because Apple never holds the keys, it is technically unable to access the content of iCloud accounts even when served with a lawful request.

Last February, Apple [withdrew](https://therecord.media/apple-encryption-feature-off-britain) ADP for British users entirely after receiving the technical capability notice, as was first reported by The Washington Post. Although the details of the notice are not public, it appears that Apple effectively complied by withdrawing ADP for British users.

Since the initial report about the notice, Westminster officials have continued to repeatedly state they do “not comment on operational matters, including for example confirming or denying the existence of any such notices.”

Addressing Parliament earlier this year, Security Minister Dan Jarvis again declined to respond after the issue was raised by a lawmaker, and said that not doing so was “a long-standing position held by successive Governments for obvious reasons of national security.”

Those “obvious reasons” were challenged by the tribunal, as well as in comments from both within the U.K. intelligence community and from academic experts.

In a preliminary judgment last April, the tribunal cited numerous public comments by senior U.S. politicians, including President Donald Trump, that appeared to confirm the existence of the notice, with Trump comparing the British government’s move to “something that you hear about with China.”

The then-U.S. Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard [ordered a legal review](https://therecord.media/odni-gabbard-uk-apple-backdoor-request-grave-concern) of the secret directive in February 2025, and said she had a “grave concern” about its implications. In its judgment, the tribunal also referenced numerous British media reports about the notice, citing government officials as sources, as additional justification for finding against the “neither confirm nor deny” policy.

Experts, including from Britain’s own intelligence community, have argued the government’s attempts to access encrypted messaging platforms should be more transparent. Academics [described](https://therecord.media/apple-uk-back-door-request-privacy-security-experts) the Home Office's ongoing refusal to either confirm or deny the legal demand as unsustainable and unjustifiable.

Tim Stevens, the head of the King's Cyber Security Research Group at King's College London, previously told Recorded Future News that a default of no comment left the government unable to control the story when details came out.

Sir Jeremy Fleming, the former director of GCHQ, [told](https://therecord.media/intel-agencies-must-explain-what-they-do-fleming-gchq) the Munich Cyber Security Conference in 2025 that greater transparency was needed to protect the agencies’ “license to operate.”

In 2018, GCHQ technical officials argued in an essay for Lawfare that the details of any exceptional access scheme would probably become public anyway, and that criminals would not simply switch services once it was known to exist. The tribunal found that confirmation of a notice’s existence would not tell adversaries anything that they could not already find elsewhere.

Andrew Dwyer, a lecturer in information security at Royal Holloway, University of London, argued that a notice capable of removing an encryption service from millions of users is not like a live operation and should not be shielded in the same way.

The ruling does not make a finding about whether the notice issued to Apple is lawful. A substantive hearing has been scheduled for the first half of 2027.

The tribunal's April 2025 judgment, quoted again in this ruling, noted that the government can choose to withdraw or settle proceedings rather than depart from its “neither confirm nor deny” approach.

A government spokesperson said: “Terrorists, child abusers and organised crime groups cannot be allowed to operate beyond the reach of the law. The right to privacy is crucial but it should not come at the expense of the safety of the British public.

“A judge can authorise access to a criminal’s home, a parcel they’ve sent, or a car they drive. The online world is no different. These powers are subject to strict safeguards and the government never has direct access to a company’s communications systems or data.

“Apple has been helping the police and intelligence agencies pursue criminals and bring them to justice for several years, in accordance with the law. This Technical Capability Notice simply requires that they maintain the ability to do so.

“Decisions affecting the safety of the British public must be made by democratically elected governments and independent judges, not in the boardrooms of technology companies.”
