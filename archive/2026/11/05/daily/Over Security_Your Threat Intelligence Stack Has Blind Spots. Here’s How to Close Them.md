Your Threat Intelligence Stack Has Blind Spots. Here’s How to Close Them

Your SOC pays for multiple threat intelligence feeds, yet fresh malicious domains and IPs can still appear in telemetry as unknown. When feeds rely on overlapping sources, more subscriptions may deliver the same indicators while leaving emerging threats uncovered.

Analysts must fill these gaps manually, checking indicator reputations, comparing sources, and gathering evidence. Every extra lookup consumes valuable time, increases the triage queue, and puts response service-level agreements (SLAs) at risk. **While the team works to establish whether an alert is malicious, attackers gain more time to operate.**

The answer isn’t necessarily another feed. It’s **intelligence that brings fresh indicators and investigative context into your existing security workflows**, helping [SOC](https://any.run/enterprise/?utm_source=anyrunblog&utm_medium=article&utm_campaign=intelligence-stack-blind-spots&utm_term=051126&utm_content=linktoenterprise) and [MSSP](https://any.run/mssp/?utm_source=anyrunblog&utm_medium=article&utm_campaign=intelligence-stack-blind-spots&utm_term=051126&utm_content=linktomssp) teams detect threats earlier and investigate with less manual effort.

## Better Threat Coverage Starts at the Source

Multiple threat intelligence feeds can give a SOC more indicators without improving visibility into emerging threats. When providers rely on overlapping public reports and third-party sources, additional subscriptions may repeat information the team already has while leaving fresh malicious infrastructure uncovered.

**The problem is not only what intelligence contains, but when it becomes available.** An indicator can be accurate and useful for retrospective analysis yet arrive too late to help detect an attack as it unfolds.

Public reports and shared IOC lists remain valuable, but they often document attacks after the activity has been observed and investigated. By the time findings are published, processed, and redistributed through feeds, attackers may have already abandoned the infrastructure described and moved to new domains, URLs, or command-and-control servers.

**For [SOC](https://any.run/enterprise/?utm_source=anyrunblog&utm_medium=article&utm_campaign=intelligence-stack-blind-spots&utm_term=051126&utm_content=linktoenterprise) and [MSSP](https://any.run/mssp/?utm_source=anyrunblog&utm_medium=article&utm_campaign=intelligence-stack-blind-spots&utm_term=051126&utm_content=linktomssp) teams, this creates a gap between malicious activity visible in telemetry and the intelligence available to recognize it.** Analysts must manually investigate unfamiliar indicators, [establish their reputation](https://any.run/cybersecurity-blog/streamline-your-soc/), and gather context, adding work to the triage queue while delaying detection and response.

**More feeds don’t necessarily mean fresher intelligence or better coverage.** Closing these gaps requires data collected closer to actual threat activity, with fresh indicators and investigative context that complement existing sources.

## How Outdated Intelligence Puts Pressure on Your SOC

When intelligence arrives late, the SOC has to compensate with its own time and resources. Security tools have fewer [relevant indicators](https://any.run/cybersecurity-blog/threat-monitoring-ti-feeds/) to match against, analysts face more uncertainty, and response decisions take longer. Time spent researching one unfamiliar domain is time unavailable for the next alert.

The impact extends across three connected areas of security operations: threat detection, analyst workload, and incident response.
