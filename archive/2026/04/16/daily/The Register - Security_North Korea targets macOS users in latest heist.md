## Social engineering: 'low-cost, hard to patch, and scales well'

![icon](/design_picker/d518b499f8a6e2c65d4d8c49aca8299d54b03012/graphics/icon/vulture_red.svg)
[Jessica Lyons](/Author/Jessica-Lyons "Read more by this author")

Thu 16 Apr 2026  //
18:20 UTC

![](/design_picker/d2e337b97204af4aa34dda04c4e5d56d954b216f/graphics/icons/social_share_icon.svg)

North Korean criminals set on stealing Apple users' credentials and cryptocurrency are using a combination of social engineering and a fake Zoom software update to trick people into manually running malware on their own computers, according to Microsoft.

Redmond's threat intelligence team tracks the Pyongyang-backed crew as [Sapphire Sleet](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/04/16/dissecting-sapphire-sleets-macos-intrusion-from-lure-to-compromise/) (aka [APT38](https://attack.mitre.org/groups/G0082/)). The [Lazarus Group](https://www.theregister.com/2026/02/24/north_koreas_lazarus_group_healthcare_medusa_ransomware/) offshoot has been in business since at least 2020, and primarily targets the finance sector to steal cryptocurrency wallets and intellectual property related to cryptocurrency trading and blockchain platforms.

These attacks begin with social engineering. The crew creates [fake recruiter profiles](https://www.theregister.com/2025/10/16/norks_abuse_blockchains_to_scam/) on social media and networking platforms like LinkedIn and then reaches out to finance professionals with phony job opportunities before scheduling a technical interview - that's the delivery mechanism for the malware.
