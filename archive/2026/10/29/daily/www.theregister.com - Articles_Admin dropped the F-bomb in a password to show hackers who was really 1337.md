Welcome back to PWNED, in which The Reg offers cautionary tales from folks who’ve left their doors wide open to attackers and lived to tell the tale. This week’s misadventure involves unpatched older machines and a password that was supposed to send a message to crims.

Our story comes courtesy once again of leading red teamer and pen tester Dahvid Schloss of [Suzu Labs](https://suzulabs.com/home-suzu-labs).

A client asked Schloss to visit a factory and check the servers there for security holes. The company had been hacked six months prior, made some adjustments, and wanted to make sure that they were bulletproof now.

REG AD

Schloss' mission, which he chose to accept, was to get domain admin rights.

REG AD

Schloss drove over to the factory where he noticed a number of Windows 7 servers were connected to the CNC milling machines, even though Windows 10 was by then Microsoft's supported desktop OS. This was to be expected, as companies with proprietary software often use legacy OSes for fear of breaking apps.

At first, Schloss had a hard time breaking in because most of the servers were not connected to the domain. So there was no point in his running the EternalBlue zero-day that would have let him in.

“So I've been looking through these and most of them had been disconnected from the domain, so it's the right move,” he said. “Like, yeah, you could run EternalBlue on the network, but you really weren't going to be able to get anywhere with it, cuz you get on the device and what you find: you dump LSASS [the Windows Local Security Authority Subsystem Service], you’re like ‘All right, there’s local admin creds, but the local admin creds on those devices were different from the local admin creds on the majority of workstations.”

Finally, Schloss went onto one of the servers that was connected to milling machines and found that it was connected to the network and on the domain. It was vulnerable to EternalBlue and he went and dumped LSASS and found a pair of creds from WDigest, one of which looked like an admin account.

But Schloss thought that it must not be a legitimate admin account when he read the password : “heyhackergof\*\*\*yourself,” but written in leet speak [[l337 5p34k](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leet)].

The resulting string was a strong password, but Schloss assumed that the admins were playing a game with him and left this credential there for him to find. Schloss assumed it wouldn't work and sat on it for a week.
